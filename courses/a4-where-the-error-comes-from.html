Where the error comes from

Lanternfish's classifier is wrong on 12 percent of its dev set. That one number hides three: the part no model could remove, the part the model fails to learn from the data it has, and the part that comes from not generalising beyond what it saw. Three numbers a team already has, training error, dev error and a human's error on the same task, split the 12 into 2, 1 and 9. Each of those funds a different family of projects, and a team that funds the wrong family does excellent work for a quarter and moves nothing.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A diagnosis from three numbers any team can produce by Friday: training error, dev error, and the best estimate of how well a person does the same task. The two subtractions that turn them into avoidable bias and variance. Which family of projects each diagnosis funds, so a sprint plan can be checked against the diagnosis in one glance. And the reason a leader should be suspicious of any change that moves two of the three numbers at once.

LiveWhy one error number is not enough to fund anything3 min▶

"The model is 12 percent wrong" is a fact, and it is not a decision. Twelve percent because the model is too simple to fit the pattern is fixed one way; twelve percent because the model fits the training data beautifully and falls apart on anything new is fixed the opposite way; and some of the twelve is not the model's fault at all, because the frames are too dark for anyone to read. Three different problems, one number, and every proposal in the backlog silently assumes one of the three.

The split takes two subtractions and no new experiments. A team that does the arithmetic funds the source that is actually large. A team that skips it funds whichever source the loudest proposal assumed, and finds out in three months.

Real world

Lanternfish's first quarter went on a bigger model with a longer training run, because a bigger model is what the team knew how to build. Training error fell from 3 percent to 2. Dev error stayed at 12. The gap the team had been paying for was never between the model and the data it saw; it was between the data it saw and the field.

Self-studyThe vocabulary for tonight4 min▶
  • Training error - how often the model is wrong on the data it learned from. Lanternfish: 3 percent.
  • Dev error - how often it is wrong on the dev set, data it did not learn from, drawn from the traffic the field will judge. Lanternfish: 12 percent.
  • Human-level error - how often a competent person is wrong on the same task with the same inputs. Lanternfish's field biologists: 2 percent on the same frames.
  • Optimal error rate - the error no model could get below, because some inputs do not carry the answer. Also called unavoidable bias. In practice estimated by human-level error.
  • Avoidable bias - training error minus the optimal error rate. What the model fails to learn even from the data it has.
  • Variance - dev error minus training error. What the model fails to carry over from the data it saw to the data it did not.
  • Bias, loosely - the training error as a whole, unavoidable and avoidable together. Only the avoidable part is a target.
Part 1 · covers chapters 20, 22 and 33

Bias and variance: the two big sources of error

10 min live

Chapter 20 of Machine Learning Yearning is titled "Bias and Variance: The two big sources of error", and the discipline treats them as a diagnosis rather than a piece of theory: two numbers that say where the error lives, computed from figures the team already has. Chapter 22 adds the third piece, the part of the error that no model can remove, and chapter 33 explains why a human doing the same task is the best available estimate of it.

LiveThe decomposition, in the book's own example4 min▶

Chapter 22 works the arithmetic on a system with 30 percent dev error. Some of that error is structural: the inputs are so noisy that the best possible system would still be wrong 14 percent of the time. That 14 is the optimal error rate, also called "unavoidable bias", and no project on any backlog can touch it. The next slice is avoidable bias, 1 percent, "calculated as the difference between the training error and the optimal error rate": the model is wrong on its own training data 15 percent of the time, and 14 of that was unavoidable, so 1 point is the model's own failure to learn. The rest is variance, dev error minus training error, which is 30 minus 15: everything the model learned on its training data and failed to carry over.

The same three subtractions on Lanternfish: training error 3, dev error 12, human-level 2. Unavoidable bias 2. Avoidable bias 3 minus 2, which is 1. Variance 12 minus 3, which is 9. Nine points of variance against one of avoidable bias. The diagnosis is variance, and it took thirty seconds once the three numbers were on the table.

Real world

Lanternfish had all three numbers for a year. Training error was on the dashboard, dev error was on the dashboard, and the field biologists' accuracy on the same frames had been measured once for a grant application. Nobody had subtracted. The quarter spent on a bigger model attacked the 1 and left the 9 alone.

LiveWhy a human is the yardstick3 min▶

The optimal error rate cannot be measured directly; nobody has the perfect model to read it off. Chapter 33 gives the practical substitute: on tasks people do well, human-level performance is the best available estimate of it. If field biologists are wrong 2 percent of the time on the same infrared frames, then roughly 2 percent of those frames do not carry enough information to answer, and a model that reaches 2 percent has reached the floor.

Human-level error does two jobs at once. It tells the team how much of the training error is avoidable, which is the first subtraction. And through that, it tells them which of the two sources is worth attacking: a model at 3 percent training error against a 2 percent human is nearly done learning from its data, so the 9 points of dev error above that cannot be a learning problem. Without the human number, 3 percent training error looks like room to improve, and a team will spend the quarter improving it.

Self-studyGetting an honest human-level number4 min▶
  • Same inputs the model sees. A biologist with the full video clip and a map of the camera site does better than one with the single frame the model gets. The yardstick has to be measured on the model's inputs, or it will read too low and make avoidable bias look larger than it is.
  • Competent people, not average ones. The optimal error rate is a floor, so the estimate should come from whoever does the task best. A team of specialists agreeing beats one person guessing.
  • A sample of the dev set, not a different set. Human-level error on daytime web photos says nothing about night infrared frames. Fifty to a hundred dev frames, scored by hand, is usually enough to place the number within a point.
  • Write down the date. A human-level estimate from a grant application two years ago was measured on different cameras. It is a number, and it is not the yardstick.
  • When people are bad at the task, a stock forecast, a click prediction, human-level error stops being a good proxy for the optimal error rate and the decomposition gets harder to read. Say so rather than pretending the number means what it means for species in a photo.
Part 2 · covers what each diagnosis funds, and orthogonalization

Each diagnosis funds a different project

9 min live

The reason to split the error is that the two sources are fixed by different work, and the work for one does little for the other. A backlog is a list of projects, each of which assumes a diagnosis. Reading the backlog against the actual diagnosis is the leader's job, and it takes one glance once the families are known.

LiveWhat a bias diagnosis funds, and what a variance diagnosis funds4 min▶

Chapter 25 lists the projects that reduce avoidable bias, and they are all ways of letting the model learn more from the data it already has: a bigger model, training for longer, better features. Chapter 27 lists the projects that reduce variance, and they are ways of stopping the model from learning the training data too specifically: more training data, regularisation, a simpler model. The two lists are close to opposites, and that is the point. A bigger model helps bias and can hurt variance; more data helps variance and does nothing for bias.

  • Bias diagnosis: the model is wrong even on what it trained on, by more than a person would be. Fund the chapter 25 family. Ceiling: the avoidable bias. For Lanternfish, 1 point.
  • Variance diagnosis: the model is nearly as good as a person on its training data and much worse on new data. Fund the chapter 27 family. Ceiling: the variance. For Lanternfish, 9 points.

The names are enough for the decision. Whether regularisation is done one way or another, what a "bigger model" means for a given architecture, is technique, and the intro machine learning and deep learning courses own it. The leader's question is only whether the sprint is drawing from the family the numbers point at.

Real world

Lanternfish's quarter on the bigger model was a bias project against a variance diagnosis. It worked, in the sense that training error fell by a point. It could not have moved dev error by more than the single point of avoidable bias, and the sprint plan had promised five. The next quarter's plan, more night frames from the deployed cameras and a smaller model, was drawn from the other list, and it had nine points of room.

LiveOne knob per problem3 min▶

Andrew Ng's Structuring Machine Learning Projects course, week 1, gives the design principle behind the two lists a name: orthogonalization. This is course material, not a chapter of the book. The idea is that a well-run project has one knob per problem: a set of changes that move training error and leave variance alone, and a different set that move variance and leave training error alone. Then a change can be judged by the one number it was meant to move.

The practical consequence for a leader is a suspicion. A proposed change that moves two of the three numbers at once, training error down and dev error up, say, is a change nobody can reason about, because there is no way to say which effect was intended and whether the trade was worth it. Ask for changes that pull one knob, measure the one number, and keep or discard on that.

Self-studyThe honest limit of the three numbers4 min▶
  • Dev error is only as good as the dev set. If the dev set is drawn from the wrong traffic, the variance number is the distance to the wrong place. Practitioner session 1 builds the set; a leader's check is one question: if the model were perfect on this dev set, would the field be satisfied?
  • Human-level error is only as good as the measurement. Measured on different inputs, on an easier sample, or by average rather than competent people, it shifts the boundary between unavoidable and avoidable bias and can turn a variance diagnosis into a bias one.
  • The decomposition says which source, not which cause. Nine points of variance does not say whether the fix is more night frames or fewer parameters. That is why session 3's spreadsheet comes first: the sheet says what the errors are, the three numbers say whether more learning or more data would remove them.
  • A model can have both problems. Avoidable bias 4 and variance 5 is two doors to walk through, in order of size. The numbers still decide the order; they just do not let the team stop after one.
  • The numbers move after every project. Recompute all three when the model changes. A variance project that worked will have shrunk the gap, and the next diagnosis may be different.
Build-along

Three numbers, one diagnosis

★ 18 min · everyone builds

Bring one model your team owns and whatever dashboard reports on it. If you have nothing to hand, use Lanternfish: training error 3, dev error 12, field biologists at 2 on the same frames. The exercise is two subtractions and one comparison with the sprint plan, and it should not take longer than the arithmetic.

Get three numbers for one model. Training error, dev error, and the best estimate of human-level error on the same task with the same inputs. If the third does not exist, the first finding of the session is that it should, and someone scores fifty dev examples by hand this week. Write the date next to each number.

Compute avoidable bias and variance. Avoidable bias is training error minus human-level error. Variance is dev error minus training error. Whatever is below the human-level line is unavoidable and comes off the table. For Lanternfish: 3 minus 2 is 1, 12 minus 3 is 9, and 2 is nobody's project.

Write which source is bigger and which family that funds. One sentence. "Variance 9 against avoidable bias 1: fund the more-data, regularisation, simpler-model family." The sentence is the diagnosis, and it goes at the top of the sprint plan.

Check whether the current sprint is attacking that source or the other one. Read each project in the plan and mark it bias-family or variance-family from its own description. Count how many are on the side the numbers point at. A plan with most of its weeks on the smaller source is the quarter about to be wasted, and it is visible before it starts.

★ The three-number diagnosis, as the header of the sprint plan Lanternfish species classifier - diagnosis, dev set of 1,000 night and day frames training error 3 percent (last full run) dev error 12 percent (same run, dev set) human-level error 2 percent (two field biologists, 80 dev frames, same single frame the model sees) unavoidable bias 2 below the human line, nobody's project avoidable bias 1 training minus human-level variance 9 dev minus training diagnosis: variance, nine to one fund: more data from the deployed cameras, regularisation, a simpler model (chapter 27 family) do not fund this quarter: bigger model, longer training, better features (chapter 25 family, 1 point of room) current sprint: 3 projects, 11 weeks, all bias-family -> mark for discussion before planning closes

The diagnosis does not pick the project; it rules out half the backlog. Nine points of variance says the fix is on the more-data side of the list, and session 3's spreadsheet says which data: night frames, of which the training set has too few. Both instruments together choose the project. Either alone chooses a direction.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. For one model your team owns, get the three numbers on one page with the date each was measured. If the human-level number is missing or older than the current dev set, arrange for fifty dev examples to be scored by a competent person this week.
  2. Do the two subtractions and write the one-sentence diagnosis. Put it at the top of the next sprint plan where the sponsor will read it.
  3. Label every project in the current plan bias-family or variance-family from its own description. Count the weeks on each side and compare with the diagnosis.
  4. Find the last change the team shipped that moved both training error and dev error. Write down which effect was intended, and whether anyone could say at the time.
